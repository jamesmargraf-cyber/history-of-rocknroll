What Does It Say? — Lesson 1: Overview

History of Rock 'n' Roll · Unit 2

Music as Message

Popular music is one of the most powerful vehicles humans have ever invented for communicating ideas, emotions, and demands. But why? What was it that made Chuck Berry's songs — sung by a 30-something Black man in segregated America — teenage anthems? What made Dylan's protest songs protest anthems? What makes Kendrick Lamar's social commentary or Taylor Swift's confessional storytelling compelling to millions?

In this unit we move from how music sounds to what music means. We'll explore how musicians use songs to say things that are difficult — or impossible — to say any other way.

Essential Question

Why do humans use music to say things they can't just say?

Concept 1
Three Channels of Communication
Every song sends its message through three channels at once

A song doesn't communicate through words alone. Three channels work together — and sometimes against each other — to shape what a song actually says. You already know the first one well.

🎸
Music
Tone, mood, tempo, dynamics, instrumentation — the emotional atmosphere that surrounds and shapes everything else.
Unit 1 — What Does It Sound Like?
📝
Lyrics
The explicit message — what the song literally says. Characters, images, arguments, stories, demands.
Unit 2 — What Does It Say?
🌍
Context
Who made it, when, for whom, under what conditions. The same song can mean different things in different moments.
Unit 3 — Why Was It Said?
Key Idea

These three channels work together — and sometimes against each other. A love song played at a funeral means something different than the same song played at a wedding. The music, the words, and the moment are all part of the message.

🎧 Listen & Compare — Same Lyrics, Different Message

Bob Dylan wrote "All Along the Watchtower" in 1967 as a sparse acoustic folk song. Jimi Hendrix released his version the same year — same lyrics, but the music transforms the message entirely. Dylan was so taken with Hendrix's version that he has played it that way ever since. In his own words: "I liked Jimi Hendrix's record of this and ever since he died I've been doing it that way. Strange how when I sing it, I always feel it's a tribute to him."

Concept 2
Songs Are Arguments
Music isn't just entertainment — it makes claims about the world

Every song takes a position. It might argue that love is worth the pain, that the government has failed its people, that this moment deserves to be celebrated, or that someone's death needs to be remembered. Even a song that seems "just fun" is making an argument — that joy matters, that dancing is enough, that right now is worth inhabiting.

When you start listening for what a song is arguing, you hear it differently. The music stops being wallpaper and starts being a voice.

Think About It

Think of a song that said something you needed to hear. What was it arguing? Why couldn't a conversation have done the same thing?

Background
A Long Tradition
Rock inherited centuries of song-as-message — here are the main bloodlines

Before rock, before pop radio, before recording technology — humans were already using song to tell stories, demand justice, and mark the moments that mattered. Rock didn't invent any of these traditions. It absorbed them, electrified them, and sent them to a mass audience.

This is a map of that territory. We'll come back to each of these traditions in depth as the unit unfolds — for now, the goal is to recognize them when you hear them.

Ballad
Story in song
A ballad tells a story — usually with characters, a sequence of events, and an emotional payoff. The tradition stretches back to medieval oral poetry; wandering performers carried news and history from town to town through song. Ballads are often slow, verse-driven, and emotionally intense. Tragedy is a favorite subject.
Classic: "House of the Rising Sun" — The Animals (1964)  ·  Modern: "Fast Car" — Tracy Chapman (1988)

"House of the Rising Sun" — The Animals (1964) · A first-person story of ruin — the speaker warns others away from the life that destroyed them

Love Song
Connection & longing
The most universal tradition in human music. Love songs express connection, desire, heartbreak, jealousy, devotion, and loss. They give voice to feelings that resist direct speech — and let listeners project their own experiences onto someone else's words. The love song is endlessly adaptable: the same tradition produces "I Want to Hold Your Hand" and "Someone Like You."
Classic: "I Will Always Love You" — Dolly Parton (1974)  ·  Modern: "Someone Like You" — Adele (2011)

"I Will Always Love You" — Dolly Parton (1974) · Written as a professional farewell to her mentor Porter Wagoner — not a romantic partner. Parton: "It was kind of my goodbye song to Porter."

Murder Ballad
Dark storytelling
A specific — and surprisingly old — branch of the ballad tradition. Murder ballads tell stories of crime, violence, and consequence, often from the killer's, victim's, or witness's perspective. They function as moral warnings and a way of processing real violence through narrative. The genre spans folk, rock, country, and hip-hop.
Classic: "Hey Joe" — Jimi Hendrix (1966)  ·  Modern: "Goodbye Earl" — Dixie Chicks (1999)

"Hey Joe" — Jimi Hendrix (1966) · A man on the run after shooting his partner — told without judgment, which makes it more unsettling

Protest Song
Speak truth to power
Protest songs challenge authority, bear witness to injustice, and demand change. Their roots run deep in American history — from spirituals to labor anthems to the Civil Rights movement. Rock absorbed this tradition through folk, soul, punk, and hip-hop. A protest song can make you feel history in ways that journalism cannot.
Classic: "A Change Is Gonna Come" — Sam Cooke (1964)  ·  Modern: "Alright" — Kendrick Lamar (2015)

"A Change Is Gonna Come" — Sam Cooke (1964) · Inspired by multiple events including being turned away from a whites-only Holiday Inn in Shreveport, Louisiana (Oct. 1963) and hearing Dylan's "Blowin' in the Wind." Cooke was killed two weeks before the single was released.

Ceremony Song
Mark the moment
Some songs exist to mark moments — weddings, victories, graduations, locker rooms. The ceremony song transforms an ordinary moment into a significant one. It tells a group: we are here, together, and this counts. The tradition is as old as ritual itself.
Classic: "We Are the Champions" — Queen (1977)  ·  Modern: "All I Do Is Win" — DJ Khaled ft. T-Pain (2010)

"We Are the Champions" — Queen (1977) · Freddie Mercury: "I was thinking about football when I wrote it. I wanted a participation song, something that the fans could latch on to."

The Big Picture

These traditions predate rock by centuries. Rock didn't invent them — it inherited them, plugged them in, and turned up the volume. As we move through this unit, we'll look closely at how different traditions and formats work better for different kinds of messages.
